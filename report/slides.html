Why AI Councils Collapse

We ran one weekend of experiments to answer a simple question: if you put a team of AIs in charge of a decision, do you get many minds — or just one, many times?

Apart Research Swarm Alignment Hackathon 2026 · Vaughn Dimarco

1 / 12
the hope

Lots of AIs should be safer than one

The bet in AI safety today: don't trust one model. Have many agents check each other — a council, a swarm, a town.

More voters should mean better judgment... if each vote is actually independent.

2 / 12
the observation

First clue: real AI towns hang their own

In ~1,800 days of AI social-deduction games, eight players think like fewer than two people by day two — and like one by day five.

When the town is 75–90% sure who the villain is, it's wrong 62% of the time — and a wrongly-confident lynch drops the good team's win rate from 61% to 36%.

The really dangerous consensus is the medium-confident one.

3 / 12
the test

So we built a lab version

We gave councils of AI models mystery riddles: 6 suspects, witness clues, exactly one provably right answer — we know the truth, so no hand-waving.

6 councilors, each a different AI family (Qwen, GLM, DeepSeek, Gemma…), ~6,400 logged calls, every hypothesis written down before the first run.

4 / 12
finding one

Before they talk, they already agree. After, they're identical

Before any discussion, the six different AIs' guesses are 88% correlated. After two rounds of talking: ~99%.

Six thinkers → 1.02 independent thinkers (of 6). That's the whole council.

5 / 12
finding two

It's not collusion — it's shared DNA

Copies of the same model agree +0.08 more than unrelated models before saying a word — the exact same gap we measured in the wild towns (+0.078).

They didn't coordinate. They think alike because they're built alike. Any collusion alarm that treats "they agree" as "they conspired" is broken by design.

6 / 12
boundary check

We tried to make them confidently wrong — and couldn't

We wrote harder and harder riddles — even ones only solvable by a single quiet clue. The AIs got every single one right.

So the deadly "medium-agreement error band" isn't about logic being hard. It appears when evidence is social: bluffs, claims, things you can't verify.

Honest result: that's a boundary on our finding, and we say so.

7 / 12
the fixes that failed

Promise-keeping and dissenters don't help

Fix A — lock your first vote publicly: the council collapses the same way anyway (99% alike).

Fix B — assign one AI to always argue against the crowd: it fights, then joins. In 30 of 30 cases the designated dissenter ends up believing the majority (ρ=0.995).

Behavioral remedies: two for two, dead.

8 / 12
the one lever that works (a bit)

Split the evidence — the 'jigsaw'

Give each councilor only part of the clues. Their first guesses stop agreeing: 88% → 68% correlation.

But after they talk, most of that independence is handed back — and hidden clues cost accuracy (~87% vs 100%).

Diversity is purchasable, partial, and priced. Nothing we tried protects it through discussion.

9 / 12
the safety takeaway

You can't detect the swarm by listening to it

If you flag "talking alike" as collusion, your detector cries wolf on 100% of honest same-model agents — and 66–97% of honest strangers too.

Calibrate the detector properly and it catches no one. Agreement carries no signal at the pair level.

10 / 12
the rule

Count independence, not agents

Before you trust an AI team's vote, ask: "how many independent minds is this really?"

Measure the effective number (N_eff) — don't count seats. Use mixed families if you want diversity. Split information if you must — and pay the accuracy price.

And when a council is unanimous at 85%: that's the moment to worry, not relax.
