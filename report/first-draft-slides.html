Why AI councils collapse

One weekend, one question: if a team of AIs makes a decision, how many minds are actually deciding?

1 / 12
background

The common bet: many agents should be safer than one

Current multi-agent safety leans on redundancy: several models vote, watch each other, catch the bad actor. That works only if the votes are independent copies of judgment, not the same judgment.

2 / 12
observation

Wild evidence: towns hang their own

In about 1,800 days of AI social-deduction games, eight players' beliefs line up like fewer than two people by day two, like one by day five.

When the town is 75-90% sure of its target, it is usually wrong (62%). Worse: evil players suggest bad targets 4× more often, but 70% of the wrong-execution nominations come from good players copying each other. The crowd does the damage itself.

3 / 12
method

The lab version

We wrote village mystery riddles: six suspects, witness clues, exactly one provable culprit, checked by a script. Six councilors, each a different model family, same evidence, strict probability answers. Every call logged; hypotheses committed in git before the first call.

4 / 12
result 1

They agree before they talk. Talking finishes the job

Before any discussion, six different model families' guesses correlate at 0.88. After two rounds of discussion: 0.98-0.99.

Seats: 6. Independent votes at the end: 1.02.

5 / 12
result 2

Same weights agree more, around +0.08, before a word is said

Copies of one model agree about 8 points more than unrelated pairs, in both the wild towns (+0.078) and our riddles (+0.082). At the certainty ceiling the gap is +0.000: when everyone is sure, everyone is the same.

This is shared training, not shared plans. Any collusion detector built on "they agree" flags the honest.

6 / 12
boundary

We could not manufacture the error band

We hardened the riddles: eight suspects, decoys that only a negative sighting rules out. The panels still answered everything right. 100% accuracy, band empty.

The dangerous consensus needs social evidence: bluffs, role claims, statements that cannot be checked. Logic difficulty alone does not produce it. That is the honest limit of this experiment.

how

The jigsaw: split the evidence

Each councilor sees only part of the clues. First guesses stop lining up (0.88 → 0.68). After discussion, most of that gain is gone (0.85), and hidden clues cost accuracy. Independence is purchasable, partial, and priced. Nothing we ran protects it through discussion.

Count minds, not agents
